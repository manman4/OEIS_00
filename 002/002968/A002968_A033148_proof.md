# A033148(4n+1) = 2^n · A002968(n)

## Statement

**A033148(N)** is the number of solutions of the *N*-queens problem on an $N \times N$ board that are invariant under rotation by 90°.

**A002968(n)** is the number of pairings $\lbrace (b_1,c_1),\dots,(b_n,c_n) \rbrace$ of the first $2n$ positive integers such that $b_i < c_i$ for all $i$ and the $2n$ numbers

$$b_1+c_1, \dots, b_n+c_n, c_1-b_1, \dots, c_n-b_n$$

are pairwise distinct.

**Theorem.** For every $n \ge 0$,

$$A033148(4n+1) = 2^{n}\cdot A002968(n).$$

Equivalently, since OEIS records $A260189(N) = A033148(N)/2^{\lfloor N/4\rfloor}$,

$$A002968(n) = A260189(4n+1).$$

---

## Setup and notation

Fix $N = 4n+1$ and place the board so that its centre is the origin: the cells are the pairs

$$(x,y), \qquad x,y \in \lbrace -2n,\dots,-1,0,1,\dots,2n \rbrace .$$

Let $\rho$ denote the rotation by 90°,

$$\rho(x,y) = (y,-x).$$

A placement of queens is a *solution* iff no two queens share a row ($x$-coordinate), a column ($y$-coordinate), a diagonal ($x+y$), or an antidiagonal ($x-y$). Write a solution as a set $Q$ of $N$ cells; it is *rotationally symmetric* iff $\rho(Q) = Q$.

### The centre is always occupied

If $\rho(Q) = Q$ then $Q$ is a union of $\rho$-orbits. The only cell fixed by $\rho$ is the origin, so every orbit has size $4$ except the singleton $\lbrace (0,0) \rbrace$. Since $\lvert Q\rvert = 4n+1 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the origin must belong to $Q$, and the remaining $4n$ queens form exactly $n$ orbits of size $4$.

### Orbit data

For $(x,y) \ne (0,0)$ the orbit is

$$O(x,y) = \lbrace (x,y), (y,-x), (-x,-y), (-y,x) \rbrace .$$

Reading off the four coordinate statistics of these four cells:

| statistic | multiset over $O(x,y)$ |
|---|---|
| rows $x$ | $\lbrace x, y, -x, -y \rbrace$ |
| columns $y$ | $\lbrace y, -x, -y, x \rbrace$ |
| diagonals $x+y$ | $\lbrace x+y, y-x, -x-y, x-y \rbrace = \lbrace \pm(x+y), \pm(x-y) \rbrace$ |
| antidiagonals $x-y$ | $\lbrace x-y, y+x, -x+y, -y-x \rbrace = \lbrace \pm(x+y), \pm(x-y) \rbrace$ |

Two consequences, both used repeatedly below:

1. **The row multiset equals the column multiset.** Hence for a rotationally symmetric placement the row condition and the column condition are equivalent — one implies the other.
2. **The diagonal multiset equals the antidiagonal multiset**, and it depends on $(x,y)$ only through the unordered pair $\lbrace \lvert x+y\rvert, \lvert x-y\rvert \rbrace$.

Let the $n$ orbits of a symmetric solution be $O(x_i,y_i)$, $i = 1,\dots,n$.

---

## Step 1: the row/column condition is a perfect matching

The centre queen occupies row $0$ and column $0$, so $x_i \ne 0$ and $y_i \ne 0$ for all $i$.

The rows used by the non-central queens are $\bigcup_i \lbrace \pm x_i, \pm y_i \rbrace$, and they must be exactly $\lbrace -2n,\dots,-1 \rbrace \cup \lbrace 1,\dots,2n \rbrace$, each once. This set is symmetric under negation, so the condition is equivalent to:

$$\bigl( \lvert x_1\rvert ,\lvert y_1\rvert , \lvert x_2\rvert ,\lvert y_2\rvert , \dots, \lvert x_n\rvert ,\lvert y_n\rvert \bigr) \text{ is a permutation of } (1,2,\dots,2n).$$

In other words, the $n$ unordered pairs

$$\lbrace b_i, c_i \rbrace := \lbrace \lvert x_i\rvert , \lvert y_i\rvert \rbrace , \qquad b_i < c_i,$$

form a **perfect matching of $\lbrace 1,2,\dots,2n \rbrace$**. (Note $b_i \neq c_i$ is automatic from the above.) By consequence (1), the column condition then holds automatically.

## Step 2: the diagonal condition is exactly the A002968 condition

Whatever the signs, if $\lbrace \lvert x_i\rvert,\lvert y_i\rvert \rbrace = \lbrace b_i,c_i \rbrace$ with $b_i < c_i$ then

$$\bigl\lbrace \lvert x_i+y_i\rvert , \lvert x_i-y_i\rvert \bigr \rbrace = \lbrace b_i+c_i, c_i-b_i \rbrace .$$

Indeed, replacing $(x,y)$ by $(-x,y)$, $(x,-y)$ or $(y,x)$ only permutes and negates $x+y$ and $x-y$.

The centre queen contributes diagonal value $0$ and antidiagonal value $0$; orbit $i$ contributes $\pm(b_i+c_i)$ and $\pm(c_i-b_i)$ to each. Since $b_i \ge 1$ we have $c_i - b_i \ge 1 > 0$ and $b_i + c_i > c_i - b_i$, so all these values are nonzero and no clash with the centre can occur, and no clash occurs inside a single orbit.

Therefore all diagonals are distinct **iff** all antidiagonals are distinct **iff**

$$b_1+c_1, \dots, b_n+c_n, c_1-b_1, \dots, c_n-b_n \quad\text{are pairwise distinct},$$

which is precisely the defining condition of A002968.

(No "covering" requirement appears: an $N \times N$ board has $2N-1 = 8n+1$ diagonals and only $4n+1$ queens, so distinctness is the whole story.)

## Step 3: each admissible matching yields exactly $2^n$ solutions

Fix an admissible matching $\lbrace (b_i,c_i) \rbrace_i$. For a single pair $\lbrace b,c \rbrace$ with $b < c$, the cells with $\lbrace \lvert x\rvert,\lvert y\rvert \rbrace = \lbrace b,c \rbrace$ are the eight cells $(\pm b,\pm c), (\pm c,\pm b)$, and they split into exactly two $\rho$-orbits:

$$O(b,c) = \lbrace (b,c),(c,-b),(-b,-c),(-c,b) \rbrace , \qquad O(b,-c) = \lbrace (b,-c),(-c,-b),(-b,c),(c,b) \rbrace .$$

By Step 2 the diagonal data of these two orbits coincide, so if one choice is admissible so is the other. Different pairs use disjoint sets of rows and columns, and by Step 2 their diagonal contributions are determined by the pairs alone, so the $n$ choices are independent.

Hence each admissible matching lifts to exactly $2^n$ rotationally symmetric solutions, and conversely every rotationally symmetric solution arises from exactly one admissible matching (Steps 1–2). This gives the bijection

$$\lbrace \text{rotationally symmetric solutions on } (4n+1)\times(4n+1) \rbrace \longleftrightarrow \lbrace \text{A002968 pairings} \rbrace \times \lbrace \pm \rbrace ^{ n},$$

and therefore $A033148(4n+1) = 2^n A002968(n)$. $\blacksquare$

---

## Remarks

**Relation to Ahrens.** Ahrens proved that $A033148(N) = 0$ unless $N \equiv 0,1 \pmod 4$, and that $A033148(4k+1)$ is a multiple of $2^k$. Step 3 is exactly the source of that power of $2$; the theorem identifies the quotient combinatorially.

**The case $N = 4k$.** The same argument runs in parallel. With the centre of the board at the origin the cells have half-integer coordinates; doubling them gives $x,y \in \lbrace \pm1,\pm3,\dots,\pm(4k-1) \rbrace$. There is no fixed cell, so the $4k$ queens form $k$ orbits, and one obtains

$$A033148(4k) = 2^k \cdot P(k),$$

where $P(k)$ counts perfect matchings $\lbrace (b_i,c_i) \rbrace$ of the **odd** numbers $\lbrace 1,3,5,\dots,4k-1 \rbrace$ with $b_i<c_i$ and all $2k$ values $b_i+c_i$, $c_i-b_i$ distinct. Computed values: $P(1..8) = 1, 0, 1, 4, 15, 52, 257, 1589$, matching $A033148(4k)/2^k$ for $k \le 8$.
